CLASSICAL STATEMENTS (from the standard literature; this is your only mathematical input)

Conventions. k is a field; P^2 = P^2(k) is the projective plane over k. A "cubic"
("conic", "line") is a nonzero homogeneous polynomial F in k[x,y,z] of degree 3
(resp. 2, 1), taken up to nonzero scalar multiple, or its zero locus. "C passes
through P" means F(P) = 0.

CB1. Cayley–Bacharach theorem for plane cubics (Chasles' form).
 (a) Let C1 and C2 be two cubics in P^2 over k with no common component, and suppose
     they meet in nine distinct points P1, ..., P9. If C is any cubic passing
     through P1, ..., P8, then C also passes through P9.
     [Equivalently: C is a linear combination a*F1 + b*F2 of the equations F1, F2
     of C1, C2.]
 (b) (Multiplicity version, Fulton.) Let C1, C2 be cubics with no common component,
     C1 . C2 = P1 + ... + P9 as intersection cycle (points not necessarily
     distinct, counted with intersection multiplicity), and suppose C1 is
     irreducible (or, more generally, P1, ..., P9 are simple points of C1).
     If C is a cubic with C1 . C = P1 + ... + P8 + Q, then Q = P9.

CB2. Eight-point criterion.
 (a) Let P1, ..., P8 be eight distinct points of P^2 over k such that no four
     lie on a line and no seven lie on a conic. Then the cubics passing through
     P1, ..., P8 form a pencil, i.e. the k-vector space of cubic forms vanishing
     at P1, ..., P8 has dimension exactly 2 (the eight points impose independent
     conditions on cubics).
 (b) Consequently (k algebraically closed, or more generally whenever two members
     of the pencil meet in nine distinct points): all cubics through P1, ..., P8
     pass through a uniquely determined ninth point P9 (the ninth base point of
     the pencil), which recovers CB1(a).

CB3. Pascal's theorem.
 (a) Let Q be a nondegenerate (irreducible) conic in P^2 over k, and let
     A, B, C, D, E, F be six distinct points on Q. Then the three points
         X = AB ∩ DE,   Y = BC ∩ EF,   Z = CD ∩ FA
     (intersections of the pairs of opposite sides of the hexagon ABCDEF)
     are collinear (the "Pascal line").
 (b) (Converse / Braikenridge–Maclaurin.) If six points A, ..., F, no three
     collinear, are such that the three points X, Y, Z above are collinear, then
     A, ..., F lie on a conic.

CB4. Pappus's hexagon theorem.
 (a) Let L and M be two distinct lines in P^2 over k. Let A, B, C be three
     distinct points on L and A', B', C' three distinct points on M, none of
     them equal to the point L ∩ M. Then the three points
         X = AB' ∩ A'B,   Y = AC' ∩ A'C,   Z = BC' ∩ B'C
     are collinear (the "Pappus line").
 (b) (Hexagon form.) If the six vertices of a hexagon lie alternately on two
     lines, then the three points of intersection of pairs of opposite sides are
     collinear. (This is the case of CB3(a) in which the conic degenerates to the
     pair of lines L ∪ M.)
