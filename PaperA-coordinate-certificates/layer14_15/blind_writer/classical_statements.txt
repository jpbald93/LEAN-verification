CLASSICAL STATEMENTS (from the standard literature; this is your only mathematical input)

Conventions. "Line BC" means the full (infinite) line through B and C, not the segment.
A "triangle" ABC means three non-collinear points A, B, C.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
A1. DESARGUES'S THEOREM (projective plane over a field)

Let K be a field and P^2(K) the projective plane over K.

A1(a) (Perspective from a point implies perspective from a line.)
Let ABC and A'B'C' be two triangles in P^2(K) whose corresponding vertices are
distinct (A != A', B != B', C != C') and whose corresponding sides are distinct
lines (BC != B'C', CA != C'A', AB != A'B'). Suppose the three lines AA', BB',
CC' pass through a common point O (the triangles are "perspective from the
point O"). Then the three points
    P = BC ∩ B'C',   Q = CA ∩ C'A',   R = AB ∩ A'B'
are collinear (the triangles are "perspective from a line", the axis PQR).

A1(b) (Converse / dual.) Under the same non-degeneracy hypotheses, if the
three points P = BC ∩ B'C', Q = CA ∩ C'A', R = AB ∩ A'B' are collinear, then
the lines AA', BB', CC' are concurrent.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
A2. MONGE'S THREE CIRCLES THEOREM

For two circles with distinct centres O_i, O_j and unequal radii r_i != r_j,
the external centre of similitude (external homothety centre) E_ij is the
point on line O_iO_j dividing the segment O_iO_j externally in the ratio
r_i : r_j, i.e. the centre of the homothety with positive ratio r_j/r_i that
maps the first circle onto the second. (When the circles lie outside each
other, E_ij is the common point of their two external common tangents.)

A2(a) Let three circles in the Euclidean plane have distinct centres O_1, O_2,
O_3 and pairwise unequal radii r_1, r_2, r_3. Then the three external centres
of similitude E_12, E_23, E_13 are collinear.

A2(b) (Companion form.) Under the same hypotheses, the external centre of
similitude of any one pair and the two internal centres of similitude of the
other two pairs are collinear (e.g. E_12, I_23, I_13 are collinear), where
I_ij is the point dividing O_iO_j internally in the ratio r_i : r_j.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
A3. WALLACE–SIMSON LINE

A3(a) Let ABC be a triangle in the Euclidean plane and P a point on its
circumcircle. Let D, E, F be the feet of the perpendiculars from P to the
lines BC, CA, AB respectively. Then D, E, F are collinear (the line through
them is the Simson line, or Wallace line, of P).

A3(b) (Converse.) Conversely, if P is a point such that the feet D, E, F of the
perpendiculars from P to the lines BC, CA, AB are collinear, then P lies on
the circumcircle of ABC. (So: D, E, F collinear  <=>  P on the circumcircle.)

------------------------------------------------------------------------
A4. NAPOLEON'S THEOREM

A4(a) Let ABC be any triangle in the Euclidean plane. On each side, erect an
equilateral triangle lying outside ABC (i.e. on the opposite side of that
side's line from the third vertex): BCA', CAB', ABC'. Then the centres
(centroids) of these three equilateral triangles are the vertices of an
equilateral triangle (the outer Napoleon triangle).

A4(b) (Inner version.) If instead the equilateral triangles are erected
inwardly (on the same side of each side's line as the third vertex), their
centres again form an equilateral triangle (the inner Napoleon triangle),
possibly degenerate to a point when ABC is itself equilateral.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
A5. LAMI'S THEOREM

A5. Let three coplanar, concurrent, non-collinear forces F_1, F_2, F_3, with
magnitudes |F_1|, |F_2|, |F_3|, act at a point and keep it in equilibrium
(F_1 + F_2 + F_3 = 0). Let alpha be the angle between F_2 and F_3, beta the
angle between F_3 and F_1, and gamma the angle between F_1 and F_2. Then

        |F_1| / sin(alpha) = |F_2| / sin(beta) = |F_3| / sin(gamma),

i.e. each force is proportional to the sine of the angle between the other
two.
