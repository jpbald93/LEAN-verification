CLASSICAL STATEMENTS (from the standard literature; this is your only mathematical input)

Setting. P^2(K) is the projective plane over a field K (points = 1-dim subspaces of K^3,
lines = 2-dim subspaces). A conic is the zero set C = {[x] : Q(x) = 0} of a nonzero
quadratic form Q on K^3; it is non-degenerate if Q is non-degenerate (non-singular:
not equivalent to a form in fewer than three variables) and C contains at least one point
(so C is not empty and not a line pair, double line or single point). A non-degenerate conic
contains no three collinear points. For points X != Y, XY denotes the line through them.

Pascal1 (Pascal's theorem).
  Let C be a non-degenerate conic in P^2(K) and let A, B, C', D, E, F be six distinct
  points of C (the "hexagon" A B C' D E F). Then the three points
        P = AB ∩ DE,   Q = BC' ∩ EF,   R = C'D ∩ FA
  (intersections of the three pairs of opposite sides) are collinear.
  (The line through them is the Pascal line of the hexagon.)

Pascal2 (limiting / degenerate forms — stated in the classical sources as corollaries).
  (a) Five-point form: let A, B, C', D, E be five distinct points of a non-degenerate
      conic C and t_A the tangent line to C at A. Applying Pascal1 to the hexagon
      A A B C' D E, with the side "AA" read as t_A, the points
        t_A ∩ C'D,   AB ∩ DE,   BC' ∩ EA
      are collinear.
  (b) Pappus (degenerate conic = pair of lines): if A, C', E are distinct points on a line
      l and B, D, F distinct points on a line m != l, all six different from l ∩ m, then
      AB ∩ DE, BC' ∩ EF, C'D ∩ FA are collinear.

Pascal3 (converse; Braikenridge–Maclaurin).
  If six distinct points A, B, C', D, E, F of P^2(K), no three collinear, are such that
  AB ∩ DE, BC' ∩ EF, C'D ∩ FA are collinear, then the six points lie on a
  non-degenerate conic.

Brianchon1 (Brianchon's theorem).  Assume char K != 2.
  Let C be a non-degenerate conic in P^2(K) and let a, b, c, d, e, f be six distinct
  tangent lines of C (the sides of a circumscribed hexagon). Let the vertices be
        V1 = a∩b, V2 = b∩c, V3 = c∩d, V4 = d∩e, V5 = e∩f, V6 = f∩a.
  Then the three diagonals V1V4, V2V5, V3V6 joining opposite vertices are concurrent
  (the common point is the Brianchon point).

Brianchon2 (dual form, for comparison).
  (a) Brianchon1 is the plane dual of Pascal1: under the polarity defined by C, the six
      tangent lines correspond to their six points of contact, the vertices a∩b, ... to the
      sides of the inscribed hexagon, and concurrence of the diagonals to collinearity of
      the Pascal points.
  (b) Degenerate form: if two adjacent sides coincide (a = b), their "intersection" is
      read as the point of contact of a with C, and the conclusion still holds
      (pentagon form).
