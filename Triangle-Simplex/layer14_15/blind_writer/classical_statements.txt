CLASSICAL STATEMENTS (from the standard literature; this is your only mathematical input)

Conventions. A "triangle" is non-degenerate (its three vertices are not collinear). For a triangle,
R is the circumradius, r the inradius, O the circumcentre and I the incentre. Distance always means
Euclidean perpendicular distance.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex1  (Carnot's theorem)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex1a. Let ABC be a triangle with circumcentre O, circumradius R and inradius r. Let
d_a, d_b, d_c be the signed distances from O to the side lines BC, CA, AB. The sign of d_a is
negative exactly when O and the interior of the triangle lie strictly on opposite sides of
line BC (that is, when the segment from O to its foot on BC lies entirely outside the triangle).
It is positive otherwise. d_b and d_c are signed the same way. Then
        d_a + d_b + d_c = R + r.

TriSimplex1b (equivalent trigonometric form). Since d_a = R cos A, d_b = R cos B, d_c = R cos C
(as signed quantities), the statement is equivalent to
        cos A + cos B + cos C = 1 + r/R.
In particular, if ABC is acute (O inside the triangle) all three distances are positive and
the plain unsigned distances sum to R + r.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex2  (De Gua's theorem)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex2a (trirectangular tetrahedron). Let OABC be a tetrahedron whose three edges at O,
namely OA, OB, OC, are pairwise perpendicular (a right-angle corner at O). Then
        Area(ABC)^2 = Area(OAB)^2 + Area(OBC)^2 + Area(OCA)^2.
Equivalently, with O the origin and A=(a,0,0), B=(0,b,0), C=(0,0,c), a,b,c>0:
        Area(ABC)^2 = (ab/2)^2 + (bc/2)^2 + (ca/2)^2.

TriSimplex2b (n-dimensional simplex form). Let n >= 2 and let S be an n-simplex in R^n with a
vertex O such that the n edges at O are pairwise orthogonal, for example O = 0 and the other
vertices at a_i e_i with a_i > 0, i = 1..n. Let F_0 be the facet opposite O and F_1, ..., F_n
the other facets (F_i is the facet not containing a_i e_i). Then
        vol_{n-1}(F_0)^2 = vol_{n-1}(F_1)^2 + ... + vol_{n-1}(F_n)^2.
(n = 2 is Pythagoras' theorem. n = 3 is TriSimplex2a.)

----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex3  (Viviani's theorem)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex3a. Let ABC be an equilateral triangle with altitude h, side s and h = (sqrt 3/2)s.
For every point P in the interior of ABC, the sum of the perpendicular distances from P to the
three sides equals h. In particular this sum does not depend on P.
TriSimplex3b (common extension). The statement also holds for P on the boundary of the triangle.
For an arbitrary point P of the plane it holds if the distances are signed: positive on the
same side of a side line as the triangle, negative on the other side.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex4  (Japanese theorem for cyclic polygons)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex4a (general). Let P be a convex polygon inscribed in a circle, i.e. a convex cyclic
polygon. Triangulate P by non-crossing diagonals into triangles whose vertices are vertices
of P. Then the sum of the inradii of the triangles is the same for every such triangulation.
TriSimplex4b (quadrilateral case). Let ABCD be a convex quadrilateral inscribed in a circle.
Then
        r(ABC) + r(ACD) = r(ABD) + r(BCD),
where r(XYZ) denotes the inradius of triangle XYZ. (Companion statement, often called the
Japanese theorem for cyclic quadrilaterals: the incentres of ABC, BCD, CDA and DAB are the
vertices of a rectangle.)

----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex5  (Euler's triangle formula)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex5a. In any triangle with circumcentre O, incentre I, circumradius R and inradius r,
        OI^2 = R(R - 2r) = R^2 - 2Rr.
TriSimplex5b (Euler's inequality, corollary). R >= 2r, with equality if and only if the
triangle is equilateral.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
TriSimplex6  (Poncelet's porism for triangles; Chapple-Euler closure)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Setting. Let Gamma be a circle with centre O and radius R, and gamma a circle with centre I
and radius r, where gamma lies in the interior of Gamma (so r < R and d = |OI| < R - r).
A triangle "is interscribed" if it is inscribed in Gamma (all three vertices on Gamma) and
circumscribed about gamma (all three side lines tangent to gamma, with gamma its incircle).

TriSimplex6a (porism / closure). If there is one triangle inscribed in Gamma and circumscribed
about gamma, then every point of Gamma is a vertex of such a triangle. Concretely: from any
point X on Gamma draw the two tangents to gamma. Let them meet Gamma again at Y and Z. Then
the line YZ is also tangent to gamma, so XYZ is inscribed in Gamma with incircle gamma.
TriSimplex6b (closure criterion, with converse). A triangle inscribed in Gamma and
circumscribed about gamma exists if and only if
        d^2 = R^2 - 2Rr.
("Only if" is Euler's formula TriSimplex5a. "If" is its converse, due to Chapple and Euler:
if d^2 = R(R-2r), then for every X on Gamma the construction in 6a closes up.)
Conversely to 6a: if d^2 != R^2 - 2Rr, then no point of Gamma is a vertex of such a triangle
(the tangent construction never closes after three steps).
